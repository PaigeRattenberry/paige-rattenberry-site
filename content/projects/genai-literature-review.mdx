## Why I wrote it

I wrote this review in April 2024. Generative AI was moving into banking, healthcare,
autonomous systems and connected devices, and the models underneath it were black boxes: nobody
could say in advance how one would react to an input it had not seen, which means failures and
unwanted biases arrive without warning. The review asks what interpretability research had to
offer against that, and how much of it was in use.

It is a literature review, a single-author survey that draws on <MetricStat value="14" /> papers.
Every framework described below is the work of the authors the review cites. What is mine is
the reading, the synthesis and the conclusion. The full text, with its reference list, is linked
in the facts column.

## The failure modes

The review starts from the ways these systems go wrong. It names hallucination,
misgeneralization and bias. Prompt injection manipulates a language model into
unintended actions or malicious output, and the review calls it a growing concern as more
applications are built on those models. Deepfakes and disinformation threaten whole information
ecosystems with content that is convincing and fabricated. Confidential data leakage sits beside
them in the conclusion's list of threats.

Its argument is that deploying these models calls for governance and responsible-AI practice
whose defences are robust, comprehensive and multi-faceted, and that evolve alongside frontier
AI. Interpretability and explainability are where the review looks for that.

## Interpretability and explainability

The review separates the terms. Interpretability, as it uses the word, is the extent to which
cause and effect can be observed in a system: a user can follow the model's reasoning and
verify that it is making decisions for the right reasons, which is what trust and debugging
need. Explainability is the ability to describe a system's internal mechanics, parameters and
architecture, which is what improving a model needs.

That is the distinction I used then; the review states it without citing a source for it.
Other literatures use the same words the other way round, with interpretability meaning the
study of a model's internals. The distinction matters more than the labels: checking that a
system is right for the right reasons is a different job from describing its machinery, and a
method can do one without the other.

The review also states the tension plainly, from a chart in one of the surveys it cites: the
model families that are easiest to explain, such as decision trees, have the lowest predictive
performance, and deep networks are the reverse. Another of its surveys found that explainable
AI lacked uniform measurements and evaluation procedures, which is the thread the conclusion
picks up.

## What the field was proposing

The rest of the review reports what researchers were proposing at the time. In the order the
review takes them:

- A defence framework against deepfakes, misinformation and disinformation that combines
  detection algorithms, cross-platform collaboration and policy with multi-modal analysis,
  digital watermarking and machine-learning-based authentication.
- INTERACTION, a generative explanation framework for natural-language inference that first
  explains and predicts a label, then generates diverse evidence, because a single explanation
  does not account for the variety in how people reason in language.
- SVEAD, which compresses data with a variational autoencoder, combines an ensemble of anomaly
  detectors, and explains the result with SHAP, permutation importance and individual
  conditional expectations.
- An evaluation framework for an application's resilience to prompt injection. Simulated attacks
  are chosen for coverage and relevance, a second language model assesses each response and
  returns a confidence score with an explanation, which a conventional malicious-content
  classifier cannot do, and attacks are weighted by impact into one resilience score. Its authors
  found newer models more resilient than older ones, and needed only small adjustments to
  take in new kinds of attack.
- Galeras, a benchmarking strategy for language models that write code. Earlier benchmarks
  compared generated code with expected code and ignored confounders such as lines of code,
  token count and prompt size; evaluating with causal inference reduced that bias.
- xAI-GAN, which uses explanation systems to change how a generative adversarial network is
  trained, so that the generator gets richer feedback on why the discriminator decided as it
  did.
- SAFE, for counterfactual explanations in safety-critical settings. Generative counterfactual
  models had been altering features a user selected, which may sit nowhere near the decision
  boundary and so defeat the purpose of a counterfactual; SAFE uses saliency maps to change only
  the regions that decide the output.
- CureNet, a clinician-in-the-loop explanation tool that combines deep-learning models, a
  fine-tuned language model and attribution methods (Integrated Gradients, layer-wise relevance
  propagation, DeepLift) to draw heatmaps of the regions behind a diagnosis.
- A diagnostic framework that pairs Markov logic networks, for medical reasoning a person can
  read, with knowledge extracted by language models and a search engine.
- Group Additive Gaussian Processes, an interpretable model for tracking economic indicators
  from social-media data, with calibrated uncertainty on its predictions.
- FCEN, a fuzzy network for reinforcement learning that writes an agent's state-to-action
  mappings as if-then rules and adds a generative model to visualize what the agent has
  learned, explaining its decision logic both globally and for a single decision.

## Where it fell short

The review's own verdict is that none of these methods had been widely deployed in practice,
and that a great deal of research was still to be done. Along the way it records that the
field had no uniform way to measure an explanation, and, from the causal-inference work, that
the benchmarks used to measure the models could themselves be confounded by the way the test
was built.

## What I took from it

The review ends in a position. Before generative models are trusted with problems that matter,
standardized interpretability and explainability metrics need to be implemented and rigorously
followed, with the assessment criteria weighted differently for different application
industries and types of model. It names safety-critical and high-reliability systems as the
place this matters most, because that is where a model that predicts wrongly, or is jailbroken,
could lead to catastrophic outcomes.

My [honours thesis](/projects/interpretable-medical-imaging-thesis) had reached the same place
from the other side: an explanation method has to be scored before it is trusted. The review
is where I found that the rest of the field was short of the same thing, an agreed way to
measure.
