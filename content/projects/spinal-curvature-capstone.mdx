## The problem

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is a sideways curve and twist of the spine that appears in late
childhood or adolescence and accelerates while the child is still growing. The usual treatment
for a progressing curve is a custom brace that presses on the torso to steer the spine straighter
as it grows; when bracing fails, what remains is spinal fusion surgery.

The brace itself is fitted by hand. An orthotist starts from an X-ray and body measurements,
carves or generates a brace, trims it at a first fitting, then takes another X-ray with the brace
on and adjusts from what it shows. Nothing in that loop measures how hard the brace presses, or
where, so every adjustment rests on experience and the patient's report. Each iteration costs
time and money, and a child can grow through the window in which a brace works best before the
brace is right.

## How it started

I went looking for a capstone that would combine medicine and engineering. Through my co-op
program I had met an orthotist, and his vision of fitting braces from measured pressure rather
than judgement became the project. I pitched it to classmates, and a <MetricStat value="six-person" />
team formed around it, with the orthotist as our industry collaborator. SFU's School of
Engineering Science wrote about the project in July 2022; the article, linked in the facts
column, names the whole team and carries our photograph.

## What we built

The system is an instrument for the missing measurement: the pressure between torso and brace,
read continuously and shown to the orthotist while the brace is being fitted.

<Diagram id="capstone-pipeline" number="2" />

- **A sensing garment.** A shirt carrying an array of force sensors, worn under the brace so
  each sensor sits between torso and brace. A later revision replaced the discrete sensors with
  sheets of pressure-sensitive material on a fixed grid, so the torso under the brace is covered
  and every reading has a known position.
- **Noise-filtering firmware.** The microcontroller samples every sensor repeatedly and averages
  the readings before sending them over USB, which suppresses noise in the analog signal. The
  team replaced a generic firmware library with its own, which scanned the sensors faster and
  made temperature drift negligible.
- **A real-time heatmap on a CAD torso.** A desktop application converts the readings to
  pressure and paints them live onto a CAD model of a torso, with a legend, so the orthotist sees
  where the brace presses hardest while the patient is wearing it. A capture is saved in a plain
  spreadsheet format that design tools such as nTopology can import. The team was explicit that
  the visualization validates the data and guides the fit; it does not diagnose.

The software was built with medical-device software standards in mind, IEC 62304 for the
software lifecycle among them, and the team's own analysis placed the device in Health Canada's
lowest-risk, non-invasive class.

## Results

The team won Best Overall Project at ICAMES 2022, the International, Cultural, and Academic
Meeting of Engineering Students, held at Boğaziçi University in Istanbul in May 2022. At the
competition, presenting the project's potential future impact was my part. When SFU wrote about
the project that July, the team's next milestone was a prototype that could be tested in clinic
to begin collecting patient data.

## What was proposed, not built

The decks close with extensions, and they are only that. One was a topology-optimized, printed
brace with openings for ultrasound, so that check-ins could happen more often without X-rays.
The other was a machine-learning model trained on the pressure data the system collects, to
predict curve severity and progression and to suggest brace designs. Neither was built during
the capstone; the pressure-sensing system above is what exists.
