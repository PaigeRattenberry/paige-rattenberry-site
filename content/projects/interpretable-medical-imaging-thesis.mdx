## The question

A classifier that scores well on a medical-image benchmark may have learned the disease, or it
may have learned an artifact that travels with the label: a scanner's text overlay, a ward's
marker. Saliency maps, class activation maps among them, are supposed to reveal which, by
highlighting the pixels a prediction depended on. But an explanation method is itself a chain of
mathematical operations with assumptions of its own, and on a real dataset there is no ground
truth for what the model used, so there is no way to score whether the map is right. My honours
thesis asked how well <MetricStat value="five" /> CAM methods (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, Layer-CAM,
Eigen-CAM and FullGrad) localize the evidence a near-perfect classifier actually relies on, once
that evidence is known.

## Inducing ground truth

The way to know is to plant it. The design is not mine: it comes from Yilun Zhou, Serena Booth,
Marco Tulio Ribeiro and Julie Shah's paper "Do Feature Attribution Methods Correctly Attribute
Features?" (AAAI 2022) and the Feature Attribution Evaluation repository that accompanies it, both
of which the thesis cites. I applied it to chest X-rays. Starting from the public COVID-19
Radiography Database of chest X-rays, I reassigned every image's label at random, so nothing in the
original image could predict its new label, and then added a text watermark of random size and
position to the positive class only, saving its rectangle as a binary mask. A model that reaches
high validation accuracy on this data can only have done so by reading the watermark. A CAM that
explains that model correctly should highlight the watermark and nothing else, and the mask makes
the comparison measurable. The ground truth is induced, not annotated: no radiologist marked
anything.

## Method

I trained ResNet-34 and VGG-16 from randomly initialized weights in PyTorch on Google Colab,
then generated CAMs from each method for watermarked test images with the pytorch-grad-cam
library, targeting each network's final convolutional layer. Each CAM assigns every pixel a
score; I binarized the map at thresholds of <MetricStat value="50–95%" /> and scored each binary
mask against the ground truth by intersection over union, averaged over <MetricStat value="ten" />
watermarked test images per experiment. Paired t-tests then compared FullGrad's per-image scores
with the runner-up method's at each threshold, at a significance level of
<MetricStat value="5%" />. No new metric was defined; the point was to apply a plain, defensible
one where ground truth existed.

## What the scores showed

<IouChart id="iou-resnet34" number="2" />

Explaining ResNet-34 (Fig. 2), FullGrad's maps were the sharpest and most targeted on the
watermark, but they carried a dispersed checkerboard pattern that no other method showed.
Thresholding removed it: FullGrad's average IoU rose with the threshold to
<MetricStat value="0.53" />, the best score in the experiment, while every other method peaked
lower and earlier. Eigen-CAM led at low thresholds; Grad-CAM++ and Layer-CAM were nearly
indistinguishable at every threshold, in the maps and in the scores.

<IouChart id="iou-vgg16" number="3" />

Explaining VGG-16 (Fig. 3), FullGrad almost exclusively highlighted the watermark, with no
dispersion, and reached <MetricStat value="0.560" />, among the highest averages in any
experiment. Grad-CAM assigned no attribution at all in that run. The t-tests supported both
pictures: wherever FullGrad led, its advantage over the runner-up was statistically significant,
except at VGG-16's highest thresholds, where thresholding had removed too many pixels from every
mask.

The failures matter as much as the winners. In one ResNet-34 experiment,
<MetricStat value="four of the five" /> methods appeared to highlight the border between spine and
lung instead of the watermark; FullGrad was not centred on the watermark either, though the
watermark sat at the edge of the region it highlighted, and every score fell to near zero. In a
VGG-16 run that never learned the task, most maps were noise, though FullGrad still found the
watermark. A CAM can only be as good as the model it explains, and a method's behaviour changes with
the architecture: the same FullGrad produced different maps for the two networks.

## Why it matters

In clinics, a deep network that reads X-rays will be judged by whether people can trust it, and
saliency maps are the tool offered for that trust. My results say the tool must itself be
tested: some methods missed the planted evidence entirely, a convincing-looking map can be
wrong, and the right threshold depends on the method. FullGrad, the only method tested that
satisfies local and global attribution at once, was the most robust, precise and accurate in
these experiments, and thresholding its output made it better. That is a reason to evaluate
explanation methods against induced ground truth before deploying them, not a reason to trust
any of them unexamined. It is also the habit I have kept since: build the measuring instrument
before trusting the thing being measured.

What carries beyond this setting is the approach, not the result. Plant a feature you control,
so that there is something to score an explanation against. Report the runs where every method
failed alongside the ones that produced a winner. Expect an explanation method's behaviour to
change with the system it explains. What does not carry is the evidence. These were
convolutional image classifiers reading chest X-rays, not language models, and a class
activation map says nothing about the internals of a transformer. Whether the same approach
holds up on other kinds of model is a question this thesis does not answer.
